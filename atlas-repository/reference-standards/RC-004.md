# 1. Executive Summary

*Acer palmatum* ‘Crimson Queen’ is a compact, red-leaved Japanese-maple cultivar characterized by finely dissected foliage and an arching, pendulous to cascading growth habit. Unlike RC-002 ‘Seiryu’, which demonstrated that dissected foliage can occur on an upright tree, ‘Crimson Queen’ represents the more familiar association between dissected leaves and a low, spreading or cascading crown.

The cultivar is especially valued for retaining reddish-purple foliage through much of the growing season. The RHS describes deep reddish-purple leaves divided into seven to nine narrow, deeply incised lobes and states that the foliage develops red and orange tones in autumn. Missouri Botanical Garden identifies strong foliage-color retention as a defining cultivar characteristic.

Published size estimates vary by source and regional framework. The RHS gives an eventual height and spread of approximately 1.5–2.5 metres. Missouri Botanical Garden gives approximately 8–10 feet in height, with a wider spread commonly reaching about 10–12 feet. These figures describe horticultural expectations, not fixed biological limits.

The cultivar’s original selector, exact introduction date, earliest catalogue appearance and complete clonal history remain unresolved. A commercial historical account attributes its United States introduction to Princeton Nurseries during the 1960s, but this statement has not been verified through a primary nursery catalogue and is therefore excluded from the frozen historical core.

Missouri Botanical Garden presents the cultivar as:

> *Acer palmatum* var. *dissectum* ‘Crimson Queen’

and also recognizes:

> *Acer palmatum* Dissectum Group ‘Crimson Queen’

### Governed interpretation

The Atlas separates:

* **cultivar identity:** ‘Crimson Queen’;
* **leaf-morphology class:** dissected;
* **horticultural classification:** Dissectum Group;
* **historical botanical formulation:** *var. dissectum*.

These formulations refer to the same cultivar in the reviewed institutional evidence, but the terms are not treated as exact conceptual synonyms.

# 4. Horticultural Classification

The RHS places ‘Crimson Queen’ in its dissectum category and defines that group as having fine, feathery, deeply divided leaves with finely serrated edges.

Missouri Botanical Garden describes dissectum maples as smaller, rounded and slow-growing forms, often with cascading branching, whose leaves are deeply divided to the base.

## Controlled classification

* **leaf division:** deeply dissected;
* **leaf outline:** narrow-lobed;
* **visual texture:** fine, feathery and lace-like;
* **architecture:** pendulous, cascading or mounding;
* **foliage color class:** red to purple-red;
* **horticultural group:** Dissectum Group.

## Validation finding

‘Crimson Queen’ confirms that morphology and architecture remain separate ontology dimensions even when they frequently co-occur.

Its dissected foliage does not logically cause its cascading habit. The two traits must still be stored independently.

# 5. Whole-Plant Architecture

The RHS describes the cultivar as arching in habit and classifies the broader habit as bushy. Missouri Botanical Garden places dissected forms within a low-spreading and cascading architectural pattern and explicitly recommends using ‘Crimson Queen’ where its cascading effect can be displayed beside ponds or streams.

## Normalized architectural description

> A compact, broadly mounding Japanese maple with arching, pendulous branches that create a cascading crown.

## Controlled architecture terms

* arching;
* pendulous;
* cascading;
* low-spreading;
* broadly mounding;
* occasionally described commercially as weeping.

## Qualification

“Weeping” is acceptable as a broad horticultural descriptor but should not imply that every branch hangs vertically.

The crown may be altered by:

* graft height;
* formative staking;
* pruning;
* branch removal;
* competition;
* snow damage;
* exposure;
* and plant age.

The RHS gives:

* eventual spread: 1.5–2.5 metres;
* time to maturity: approximately 10–20 years.

## Missouri Botanical Garden estimate

## Governed synthesis

A useful North American expectation is:

* **height:** approximately 8–10 feet;
* **spread:** approximately 10–12 feet.

The cultivar often becomes wider than tall.

## Qualification

These are contextual estimates. They do not establish:

* a fixed mature maximum;
* one universal crown ratio;
* a uniform age of maturity;
* or one annual growth increment.

## Growth rate

The dissectum form is generally described as slow growing by Missouri Botanical Garden.

### Approved formulation

> Growth is generally slow, with dimensions and crown development influenced by climate, rootstock, graft height and cultivation.

# 7. Leaf Morphology

The RHS describes deep reddish-purple leaves divided into seven to nine narrow, deeply incised lobes. It identifies the group as having fine, feathery, deeply divided leaves with finely serrated edges.

Missouri Botanical Garden describes dissectum leaves as deeply cut to the base and associates them with the terms laceleaf, cutleaf and threadleaf Japanese maple.

## Normalized morphology

* simple;
* opposite;
* palmately arranged;
* usually seven to nine narrow lobes in the RHS cultivar description;
* lobes deeply divided toward the leaf base;
* secondary incision producing a fine, feathery appearance;
* serrated margins.

## Terminology governance

The following terms are related but not identical:

* dissected;
* deeply incised;
* laceleaf;
* cutleaf;
* threadleaf;
* feathery;
* Dissectum Group;
* *var. dissectum*.

# 8. Seasonal Foliage

## 8.1 Spring

The cultivar emerges with dark red, crimson or reddish-purple foliage.

The RHS identifies the foliage as deep reddish purple. Its container guidance states that the cultivar keeps its red coloration from spring until stronger scarlet autumn tones develop.

### Status

Approved

### Confidence

High

---

## 8.2 Summer

Missouri Botanical Garden identifies excellent leaf-color retention throughout the growing season as a principal cultivar characteristic. The RHS similarly distinguishes ‘Crimson Queen’ from many deep-red cutleaf maples because it retains its color through the season.

### Approved formulation

> Summer foliage generally remains reddish purple and retains its red character better than many older red dissectum forms.

### Qualification

“Color retention” does not mean that:

* every leaf remains the same color;
* no bronzing occurs;
* no green undertone develops;
* or exposure and heat have no effect.

Pigmentation may vary with:

* light;
* temperature;
* water stress;
* plant health;
* leaf age;
* and observation date.

---

## 8.3 Autumn

The RHS reports shades of red and orange. Its container guidance refers to scarlet autumn tones. Missouri Botanical Garden’s broader maple description includes combinations of yellow, red, purple and bronze, but that broader palette is not necessarily cultivar-specific in every component.

### Approved cultivar-level synthesis

> Autumn color is principally scarlet, crimson, red or orange-red, sometimes accompanied by purple or bronze tones.

### Qualification

No single exact autumn color is guaranteed every year.

---

# 9. Flowers and Fruit

The RHS describes small purple flowers in spring, followed by purple fruits. Missouri Botanical Garden reports small reddish flowers followed by samaras that ripen from late summer into early autumn.

## Governed description

* **flowers:** small, reddish to purple, appearing in spring;
* **fruit:** paired winged samaras, sometimes purple-toned.

## Diagnostic value

Low.

Flowers and samaras contribute to the full cultivar description but do not independently distinguish ‘Crimson Queen’.

---

# 10. Cultivation

## 10.1 Exposure

The RHS recommends full sun or partial shade but states that leaf color is best in partial shade. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends full sun to part shade, while advising afternoon shade in hotter portions of the growing range.

## Governed guidance

> Partial shade, particularly protection from intense afternoon sun in hot climates, generally provides the safest balance between foliage color and reduced scorch risk.

Full sun may be suitable where:

* summers are moderate;
* soil remains reliably moist;
* wind exposure is limited;
* and the plant is well established.

---

## 10.2 Soil

The RHS recommends moist but well-drained, acidic or neutral soil. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends moist, organically rich, slightly acidic and well-drained soil.

## Approved practical guidance

* avoid prolonged drought;
* avoid waterlogging;
* retain even soil moisture;
* use mulch to keep roots cool;
* favor acidic to neutral conditions;
* and avoid hot, dry planting sites.

---

## 10.3 Wind and shelter

Both RHS and Missouri Botanical Garden recommend a sheltered position or protection from strong winds.

This is particularly important because finely dissected foliage loses water rapidly and is susceptible to physical and physiological injury.

---

## 10.4 Hardiness

The RHS assigns H6. Missouri Botanical Garden lists USDA zones 5–8.

### Approved formulation

> ‘Crimson Queen’ is commonly recommended for USDA zones 5–8 and is rated H6 by the RHS.

### Qualification

Hardiness ratings do not guarantee performance against:

* late frost;
* winter wind;
* root-zone freezing in containers;
* summer heat;
* drought;
* or poor drainage.

---

# 11. Leaf Scorch and Environmental Stress

RHS guidance states that Japanese maples are susceptible to leaf scorch and that heavily dissected cultivars are particularly prone. It identifies frost, drought, waterlogging, drying winds and hot sun as major causes.

Missouri Botanical Garden specifically warns that new foliage may scorch in full sun during hot summers, particularly when soil moisture is inconsistent.

## Governed conclusion

> ‘Crimson Queen’ has a morphology-associated susceptibility to environmental leaf scorch, especially under heat, drought, hot sun or drying wind.

## Evidence scope

This conclusion combines:

* cultivar-specific Missouri Botanical Garden guidance;
* and dissected-group RHS guidance.

It must not be represented as the result of a controlled cultivar-comparison trial.

---

# 12. Potential Pests and Diseases

Missouri Botanical Garden lists potential disease problems including:

* stem canker;
* leaf spots;
* Fusarium;
* Verticillium wilt;
* Botrytis;
* anthracnose;
* and root rots.

It also lists:

* aphids;
* scale;
* borers;
* root weevils;
* and mites.

The source also notes possible late-spring frost damage and chlorosis in high-pH soil.

## Governance qualification

These are potential problems, not predictions.

The record must not imply that ‘Crimson Queen’:

* is unusually susceptible to all listed organisms;
* will inevitably develop disease;
* or can be diagnosed from one symptom without inspection.

# 14. Diagnostic Framework

## High-confidence descriptive combination

The following combination strongly supports a descriptive identification:

1. deeply dissected lace-like foliage;
2. narrow lobes divided close to the leaf base;
3. red to reddish-purple spring foliage;
4. relatively strong red-purple summer-color retention;
5. scarlet or crimson autumn foliage;
6. low, arching and cascading architecture;
7. compact size;
8. crown commonly wider than tall.

## Authentication limits

The following do not prove identity independently:

* red dissected leaves;
* cascading habit;
* compact size;
* a nursery label;
* one photograph;
* autumn crimson color;
* or resemblance to a known specimen.

Other red dissectum cultivars may overlap substantially in these traits.

# 17. Historical Provenance

The reviewed authoritative institutional sources provide limited historical information.

A commercial specialist account reports that ‘Crimson Queen’ was introduced in the United States by Princeton Nurseries during the 1960s.

## Governed disposition

This claim is retained in the Research Horizon but excluded from the approved historical core because:

* no primary Princeton catalogue was recovered;
* the precise year was not established;
* the source does not identify the selector;
* and no independent institutional confirmation was located.

## Approved historical statement

> ‘Crimson Queen’ was established in international horticulture by the late twentieth century, but its selector, exact origin, naming date and documented introduction history remain unresolved in the reviewed primary institutional corpus.

# 28. Methodological Validation

RC-004 tested the architecture against a cultivar whose identity depends on the interaction of:

* dissected morphology;
* cascading architecture;
* persistent red-purple foliage;
* seasonal pigment change;
* and similarity to multiple related cultivars.

## Principal finding

The Atlas must distinguish:

> the presence of a color trait

from:

> the duration and stability of that color through the growing season.

For ‘Crimson Queen’, “red-leaved” is less precise than:

> reddish-purple foliage emerging in spring and retaining a substantial red-purple character through summer before developing scarlet to orange-red autumn tones.

## Architecture impact

No redesign required.

## Permanent implementation refinements

1. Pigment traits must support seasonal persistence.
2. Comparative descriptors such as “better retention” require a comparison population.
3. Cascading, pendulous, arching and weeping should remain distinguishable.
4. Group-derived stress susceptibility must preserve scope.
5. Visually similar cultivars require explicit comparison status.
6. English-language names require the same historical scrutiny as transliterated names.

# 30. Concise Public Atlas Profile

## *Acer palmatum* ‘Crimson Queen’

### Crimson Queen laceleaf Japanese maple

‘Crimson Queen’ is a compact Japanese maple with finely dissected reddish-purple foliage and a graceful cascading form. Its arching branches gradually create a broad, low mound that is often wider than tall.

### Key features

* **Habit:** Arching, pendulous and cascading
* **Height:** Commonly about 8–10 ft
* **Spread:** Commonly about 10–12 ft
* **Spring foliage:** Deep red to reddish purple
* **Summer foliage:** Red-purple with comparatively strong color retention
* **Autumn foliage:** Scarlet, crimson and orange-red
* **Leaves:** Finely dissected, usually with seven to nine narrow lobes
* **Flowers:** Small and reddish to purple
* **Fruit:** Winged samaras
* **Hardiness:** Generally USDA zones 5–8
* **Exposure:** Full sun to partial shade, with afternoon shade beneficial in hot climates
* **Soil:** Moist, rich and well-drained

### Why it is distinctive

The cultivar combines three major ornamental traits:

* lace-like dissected foliage;
* a low cascading crown;
* and red-purple color that persists through much of the growing season.

### Garden use

It is particularly effective:

* beside a pond or stream;
* on a slope;
* above a low wall;
* near a patio;
* as a specimen;
* in a courtyard;
* or in a large container.

### Care note

Protect the foliage from severe drying winds and intense afternoon heat. Maintain even moisture without allowing the roots to remain waterlogged. Dissected Japanese maples are particularly vulnerable to leaf scorch when water loss exceeds root uptake.

### Identification caution

A red, cascading laceleaf maple is not automatically ‘Crimson Queen’. ‘Tamukeyama’, ‘Garnet’, ‘Inaba-shidare’, ‘Red Dragon’ and other cultivars may share much of the same broad appearance. Reliable authentication requires documented vegetative provenance.
