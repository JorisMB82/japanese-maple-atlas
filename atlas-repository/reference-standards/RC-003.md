# 1. Executive Summary

*Sango-kaku* is an upright Japanese-maple cultivar best known for the coral-pink to coral-red coloration of its younger stems and branches. This coloration becomes most conspicuous during the dormant season, when the leaves have fallen and the young branch framework is fully visible. The bark color is less pronounced in summer and is strongest on newer wood rather than on the mature trunk and oldest branches.

The cultivar forms an upright, generally vase-shaped small tree or large shrub. North American institutional sources commonly give an eventual height of about 20–25 feet and a spread of approximately 15–20 feet, while the RHS places it within a broader 4–8 metre height category and a 2.5–4 metre spread category. These are contextual horticultural estimates rather than fixed biological limits.

Its leaves are conventional palmate Japanese-maple leaves rather than the finely divided foliage of a laceleaf cultivar. Spring foliage is described across sources as pinkish yellow, yellow-green or light green; it becomes green in summer and typically turns yellow or golden yellow in autumn, sometimes with orange or reddish accents.

The name is widely interpreted as referring to coral-colored upright growth, commonly rendered in English as “coral tower,” “coral pillar” or a closely related formulation. Because the exact historical orthography, naming intent and transliteration lineage have not been recovered from a primary Japanese source, these translations remain source-provided interpretations rather than independently verified etymology.

The RHS lists:

* *Acer palmatum* ‘Cinnabarinum’;

These are source-recorded synonyms and should not be interpreted as evidence that ‘Sango-kaku’ has dissected foliage. The historical *var. dissectum* formulation is inconsistent with the cultivar’s ordinary palmate leaf morphology and requires nomenclatural investigation rather than literal morphological application.

### Status

# 7. Bark and Stem Color

## 7.1 Principal ornamental character

The cultivar’s primary distinguishing feature is its pink, coral-pink, salmon-red or coral-red young bark.

Missouri Botanical Garden states that the pink coloration is strongest on young twigs and branches, provides excellent winter contrast and is less pronounced or nearly absent in summer. The JC Raulston Arboretum describes the winter bark as brilliant coral red, intensifying in cold weather toward a fluorescent salmon tone. The RHS describes coral-red young branches.

## Approved synthesis

> Young twigs and branches develop conspicuous coral-pink to coral-red coloration, normally reaching their strongest ornamental expression during winter.

## Evidence type

Published Description and Institutional Observation

## Scope

Cultivar-specific

## Status

Approved

## Confidence

High

---

## 7.2 Seasonal expression

### Winter

The bark is most visible after leaf fall and is repeatedly described as brightest or most effective during winter.

### Summer

Missouri Botanical Garden states that the pink coloration becomes much less pronounced and may be almost absent in summer.

### Cold influence

The JC Raulston Arboretum reports that the coloration intensifies during cold weather.

## Editorial conclusion

Bark color should be represented as:

* age-dependent;
* season-dependent;
* climate-responsive;
* and concentrated on younger stems.

It must not be represented as a constant uniform red covering the entire trunk throughout the year.

---

## 7.3 Stem age

Missouri Botanical Garden specifically states that the best coloration occurs on young twigs and branches.

### Approved conclusion

> Young wood is the principal carrier of the coral coloration.

### Implication

Older stems and the mature trunk may become:

* duller;
* grayer;
* browner;
* less saturated;
* or visually subordinate to the younger branch system.

### Diagnostic consequence

A mature tree lacking vivid red on its oldest trunk is not necessarily inconsistent with ‘Sango-kaku’.

---

## 7.4 Diagnostic strength

Coral-red young stems are highly characteristic but not unique to this cultivar.

Other Japanese-maple cultivars, including ‘Beni-kawa’ and ‘Corallinum’, may also display reddish or coral-colored stems. The JC Raulston Arboretum distinguishes ‘Corallinum’ as a cultivar selected principally for coral-colored spring foliage, with less intense bark color, while specialist commercial comparisons describe ‘Beni-kawa’ as another coral-bark cultivar with darker red bark and a somewhat smaller, broader habit.

## Approved conclusion

> Coral-red young bark is strongly diagnostic within context but is not independently authenticating.

## Normalized description

* simple;

## Confidence

# 11. Cultivation

## 11.1 Exposure

The RHS recommends full sun or partial shade. NC State likewise recommends full sun to partial shade.

## Governed synthesis

> ‘Sango-kaku’ may be grown in full sun or partial shade, but suitable exposure depends on regional heat, moisture availability and wind.

### Qualification

Full-sun tolerance does not mean that every full-sun site is suitable.

Hot, dry or wind-exposed sites may increase:

* scorch;
* drought stress;
* stem injury;
* or establishment difficulty.

---

## 11.2 Soil

NC State recommends moist, well-drained soil and notes tolerance of several soil textures. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends moist, organically rich, slightly acidic and well-drained conditions. The RHS includes chalk, clay, loam and sand within its soil categories.

## Approved practical guidance

* maintain moisture without prolonged saturation;
* ensure adequate drainage;
* favor acidic to neutral conditions;
* use organic matter where appropriate;
* avoid severe drought;
* and protect the root zone from excessive heat.

## High-pH limitation

Missouri Botanical Garden warns that chlorosis may occur in high-pH soils.

---

## 11.3 Wind and frost

Oregon State recommends protection from wind. NC State reports the possibility of frost damage, especially from late frosts. Missouri Botanical Garden states that early leaf emergence may expose foliage to late-spring frost damage.

## Approved guidance

> A sheltered site is preferable where drying winds, severe winter exposure or late frost are recurrent problems.

---

## 11.4 Hardiness

NC State and Missouri Botanical Garden place the cultivar broadly within USDA zones 5–8. Oregon State identifies hardiness to USDA zone 5.

## Approved statement

> The cultivar is commonly recommended for USDA zones 5–8.

## Required qualification

A zone rating does not guarantee performance.

Local success also depends on:

* minimum temperature duration;
* freeze-thaw cycles;
* late frost;
* summer heat;
* wind exposure;
* drainage;
* establishment;
* and plant health.

# 12. Health, Pests and Disease

## 12.1 General problems

NC State lists occasional aphids, scale, maple worms, leafrollers and leafhoppers, together with anthracnose, leaf scorch and frost injury. Missouri Botanical Garden lists potential problems including stem canker, leaf spots, Fusarium, Verticillium wilt, Botrytis, anthracnose, root rots, aphids, scale, borers, bagworms, root weevils and mites.

These lists represent potential problems, not predictions that a healthy plant will develop all or any of them.

---

## 12.2 Bacterial blight susceptibility

Oregon State Landscape Plants states that ‘Sango-kaku’ is highly susceptible to bacterial blight caused by *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *syringae*. An Extension response likewise characterizes it as one of the more susceptible Japanese-maple cultivars for bacterial leaf spot and dieback.

## Governed conclusion

> Credible extension sources identify bacterial blight or bacterial dieback as a significant cultivar-level concern.

## Status

Approved with qualification

## Confidence

Moderate to high

## Qualification

The evidence consists of expert institutional guidance rather than a retrieved controlled comparative trial.

The Atlas should not convert “highly susceptible” into a quantified disease probability without supporting trial data.

---

## 12.3 Canker and injury interactions

Oregon State’s Pacific Northwest disease guidance explains that maple branch-canker fungi commonly exploit injuries associated with freezing, sunburn, drought and pruning cuts.

## Practical interpretation

Risk reduction should emphasize:

* avoiding unnecessary bark injury;
* maintaining plant vigor;
* reducing severe drought stress;
* careful pruning;
* sanitation;
* and prompt assessment of discolored, sunken or dying branches.

This is general maple pathology guidance and must not be represented as a controlled ‘Sango-kaku’-specific management trial.

---

## 12.4 Early leaf emergence

Missouri Botanical Garden notes that foliage may emerge early and therefore be vulnerable to late frost.

### Status

Approved

### Scope

Cultivar-specific published horticultural observation

# 13. Propagation and Cultivar Continuity

The Maple Society explains that named Japanese-maple cultivars are clonal selections reproduced vegetatively. The JC Raulston Arboretum states that ‘Sango-kaku’ is commonly propagated by grafting onto Japanese-maple seedling rootstock and that rooted cuttings are possible but difficult and slow.

## Approved conclusions

1. ‘Sango-kaku’ must be propagated vegetatively to preserve cultivar identity.
2. Grafting is the principal established propagation method.
3. Seedlings must not automatically retain the cultivar name.
4. Rooted-cutting propagation may be possible but is comparatively difficult.

## Identity states

Plants should be represented using evidence-sensitive identity categories such as:

* authenticated clonal material;
* documented propagated accession;
* accessioned as ‘Sango-kaku’;
* supplied as ‘Sango-kaku’;
* commercially labelled as ‘Sango-kaku’;
* probable ‘Sango-kaku’;
* tentative ‘Sango-kaku’;
* unidentified coral-bark maple;
* seedling of ‘Sango-kaku’;
* or misidentified material.

## Rejected claim

> A coral-red stem or nursery tag alone proves that a plant is ‘Sango-kaku’.

# 14. Comparative Diagnosis

## 14.1 Principal identification pattern

The most useful characteristic combination is:

1. upright, vase-shaped architecture;
2. ordinary five- to seven-lobed palmate leaves;
3. green summer foliage;
4. predominantly yellow autumn color;
5. coral-pink to coral-red young twigs and branches;
6. strongest bark color in winter;
7. weaker bark color on older wood and in summer.

This combination supports a strong descriptive identification.

It does not provide definitive clonal authentication.

---

## 14.2 Comparison with ‘Beni-kawa’

Specialist horticultural descriptions generally characterize ‘Beni-kawa’ as:

* somewhat shorter and broader;
* darker red in bark color;
* and more orange-red in autumn than ‘Sango-kaku’.

These comparative claims are useful but presently rest primarily on specialist commercial observation rather than a formal authenticated institutional comparison.

### Status

Under Review

### Approved use

The cultivar may be listed as a relevant comparison taxon.

### Not yet approved

A definitive diagnostic key separating the two cultivars.

---

## 14.3 Comparison with ‘Corallinum’

The JC Raulston Arboretum distinguishes ‘Corallinum’ as a cultivar principally noted for coral-colored spring foliage, with its reddish bark being less intense than that of ‘Sango-kaku’.

### Status

Approved with qualification

### Diagnostic value

Moderate.

---

## 14.4 Comparison with unnamed coral-bark seedlings

Because Japanese-maple seedlings are genetically variable, seedlings from coral-bark cultivars may share some visual traits without being the same cultivar. The Maple Society’s cultivar framework requires clonal continuity rather than resemblance alone.

### Governed conclusion

A seedling with red stems is not ‘Sango-kaku’ unless vegetative continuity from the cultivar is documented.

# Part II — Concise Public Atlas Profile

## *Acer palmatum* ‘Sango-kaku’

### Coral-bark Japanese maple

‘Sango-kaku’ is an upright Japanese maple grown principally for the coral-pink to coral-red color of its young branches. The coloration is normally strongest in winter, especially on newer twigs, and becomes less conspicuous on older wood and during summer.

### Key features

* **Habit:** Upright and vase-shaped
* **Height:** Commonly about 20–25 ft
* **Spread:** Commonly about 15–20 ft
* **Young bark:** Coral pink to coral red
* **Best bark season:** Winter
* **Spring foliage:** Pinkish yellow to yellow-green
* **Summer foliage:** Green
* **Autumn foliage:** Yellow to golden yellow, sometimes with orange or red accents
* **Leaves:** Palmate, usually five to seven lobes
* **Flowers:** Small and reddish
* **Hardiness:** Generally USDA zones 5–8
* **Light:** Full sun to partial shade
* **Soil:** Moist and well-drained

### Why it is distinctive

The cultivar provides strong four-season interest:

* colored new foliage in spring;
* green foliage and an upright canopy in summer;
* yellow-gold autumn color;
* and bright coral young branches in winter.

### Garden use

It is well suited as:

* a winter-interest specimen;
* a small lawn tree;
* an accent near an entry or patio;
* a courtyard tree;
* or a focal plant viewed against evergreens, masonry or snow.

### Care note

Keep the root zone consistently moist but not waterlogged, protect the tree from severe drying winds and avoid unnecessary injury to young branches. Credible extension sources identify bacterial blight and dieback as notable concerns for this cultivar, particularly where plants are stressed or injured.

### Identification caution

Bright red young stems are highly characteristic, but several other Japanese maples can also develop coral or red bark. A nursery label or bark color alone does not prove that a plant is authentic ‘Sango-kaku’.

### Historical note

The cultivar is described as a Japanese selection. Specialist secondary literature reports that it entered European horticulture through Daisy Hill Nurseries in Ireland, but the original selector, selection date and primary introduction record remain unresolved.
