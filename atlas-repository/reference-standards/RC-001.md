# 1. Cultivar identity

## Published description

The accepted contemporary horticultural name is ***Acer palmatum* ‘Bloodgood’**. Major institutional horticultural references consistently treat ‘Bloodgood’ as a cultivar of *Acer palmatum*.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS001, RC001-AS002

## Editorial synthesis

‘Bloodgood’ is treated as a clonally maintained cultivar and has historically been propagated vegetatively. Seed-derived offspring must be recorded separately and must not automatically be identified as ‘Bloodgood’, even where they resemble the parent plant.

For repository purposes, the cultivar name should be assigned only where there is reasonable evidence of vegetatively propagated cultivar material or a documented accession maintained under that identity.

The following identity classes must remain distinct:

1. documented vegetatively propagated ‘Bloodgood’;
2. material accessioned or sold under the name but not independently authenticated;
3. seedlings raised from ‘Bloodgood’;
4. unidentified red-leaved Japanese maples resembling ‘Bloodgood’;
5. individual specimens maintained under a recorded but uncertain identity.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS003, RC001-AS039, RC001-AS040

# 2. Concise cultivar description

## Editorial synthesis

‘Bloodgood’ is a large, upright-growing red-leaved Japanese maple that normally develops into a broadly rounded small tree or substantial multistemmed shrub.

It bears non-dissected, palmately divided leaves, typically with five to seven slender, pointed lobes. New foliage emerges reddish purple and normally develops a dark red-purple or wine-red appearance through much of the growing season before turning bright red to crimson in autumn.

Small purple to purplish-red spring flowers are followed by red samaras.

This combination is characteristic of ‘Bloodgood’, but it is not independently diagnostic. Other upright red-leaved cultivars can display broadly similar foliage, coloration, fruit, and growth form.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS005–AS008, RC001-AS014–AS025, RC001-AS038

# 3. Growth form and architecture

## Published descriptions

Institutional and specialist sources describe ‘Bloodgood’ using terms including:

* large shrub;
* small tree;
* bushy;
* upright;
* rounded;
* multistemmed.

These terms describe different dimensions or horticultural expressions of the plant and need not be contradictory.

## Editorial synthesis

The best controlled description is:

> **Upright growth tendency with a crown that may become broad or rounded as the plant develops.**

“Upright” primarily describes the direction of growth and branching. “Rounded” or “broad-rounded” describes the developing crown outline. “Bushy” or “multistemmed” describes one possible cultivated form.

The cultivar should not be characterized as naturally columnar, narrowly fastigiate, or persistently compact.

Crown form may be affected by:

* training;
* pruning;
* graft height;
* number of stems;
* light exposure;
* nearby competition;
* wind;
* and available growing space.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS005–AS008

# 4. Size and growth

## Published estimates

North American institutional references commonly report an eventual landscape height of approximately **15–20 feet**.

The RHS has also published an approximate eventual height near **5 metres**, or approximately 16 feet.

Some sources indicate that mature spread may become substantial and may, under some conditions, approach the plant’s height.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS009–AS011

## Editorial synthesis

These dimensions are landscape expectations, not fixed biological limits.

The repository must distinguish among:

* eventual size estimates;
* size after a stated number of years;
* measured specimen dimensions;
* regional expectations;
* nursery descriptions;
* and biological maxima.

No single value should be treated as the universal size of the cultivar.

The most defensible practical summary is:

> **Commonly reported eventual height: approximately 15–20 feet, with substantial spread possible over time.**

## Expert interpretation

Growth is best described conservatively as **slow to moderate**. The current evidence does not support one cultivar-wide annual growth rate.

Development may vary according to:

* climate;
* establishment;
* soil;
* irrigation;
* rootstock;
* grafting;
* competition;
* and pruning.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS012, RC001-AS013

# 5. Leaf morphology

## Published description

‘Bloodgood’ is a **non-dissected** Japanese maple. Typical leaves are palmately divided into **five to seven slender, pointed lobes**.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS014–AS016

## Editorial synthesis

The cultivar belongs to the broad palmatum-type rather than the dissectum or laceleaf form.

Its presently supported leaf description includes:

* palmate blade;
* usually five to seven lobes;
* slender and pointed lobe tips;
* clearly divided but non-dissected structure;
* dark red-purple pigmentation during much of the growing season.

No cultivar-specific quantitative leaf-size range is approved for the main profile at present.

## Diagnostic limitation

The available leaf traits are insufficient by themselves to distinguish ‘Bloodgood’ from all other upright red-leaved cultivars.

Reliable authentication cannot be based only on:

* lobe number;
* pointed lobes;
* red-purple foliage;
* or non-dissected leaf form.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS017, RC001-AS038

# 8. Historical provenance

## Documented historical report

‘Bloodgood’ is historically associated with Bloodgood Nursery of Long Island, New York.

Secondary institutional evidence indicates that it was sold by the nursery before the Second World War. Specialist dendrological literature also associates the cultivar with commercial distribution by Bloodgood Nurseries.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS033, RC001-AS034

## Editorial synthesis

The presently approved historical formulation is:

> **‘Bloodgood’ is historically associated with Bloodgood Nursery of Long Island, New York. Secondary institutional evidence indicates that it was sold there before the Second World War, but primary catalogue documentation has not yet been recovered.**

The evidence does not currently establish that the nursery:

* bred the cultivar;
* selected the original clone;
* imported the original plant;
* coined the cultivar name;
* or was the first organization to propagate it.

The terms below must not be used interchangeably:

* bred by;
* selected by;
* named by;
* propagated by;
* sold by;
* distributed by;
* introduced by.

## Unresolved provenance

The following remain unresolved:

* original selector;
* original location;
* mode of origin;
* date of selection;
* naming author;
* earliest use of the cultivar name;
* earliest catalogue listing;
* original propagation history;
* and first documented introduction outside the United States.

Claims that ‘Bloodgood’ was imported in 1792 or introduced immediately after the American Civil War remain rejected.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS035–AS037
**Unresolved records:** RC001-U001–U007
**Rejected records:** RC001-R001–R004

# 9. Propagation and cultivar continuity

## Documented horticultural report

Historical dendrological literature reports propagation by:

* grafting;
* and cuttings.

**Supported assertion:** RC001-AS003 and propagation evidence underlying the approved register

## Editorial synthesis

Vegetative propagation is required to reproduce the named cultivar with an expectation of clonal continuity.

Seed progeny are genetically variable and must not automatically be recorded as ‘Bloodgood’.

Vegetative propagation does not itself prove that every commercially distributed plant is authenticated. A complete identity assessment may also require:

* nursery provenance;
* accession history;
* supplier documentation;
* comparison with established material;
* or other verification.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS003, RC001-AS039, RC001-AS040

## Light and exposure

### Published recommendation

Institutional guidance places ‘Bloodgood’ within a broad range from full sun to partial shade. Other institutional guidance recommends some shade and protection from strong winds.

### Editorial synthesis

Exposure recommendations must be interpreted according to climate and site.

In cooler or more northerly conditions, the cultivar may tolerate substantial sun where soil moisture is adequate.

In hotter climates, some protection from intense afternoon sun is generally prudent.

Risk increases in sites with:

* reflected heat;
* dry soil;
* strong wind;
* abrupt exposure changes;
* or limited root volume.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS027, RC001-AS028, RC001-AS029

---

## Soil and moisture

### Broader Japanese-maple guidance applicable to ‘Bloodgood’

General *Acer palmatum* cultivation guidance favors:

* evenly moist soil;
* good drainage;
* avoidance of prolonged waterlogging;
* and protection from severe drying conditions.

### Editorial synthesis

A practical controlled recommendation is:

> **Provide moist but freely draining soil, maintain consistent moisture during establishment and dry weather, and avoid sites combining drought, reflected heat, and drying wind.**

Slightly acidic conditions are commonly recommended for Japanese maples, but no narrow cultivar-specific pH requirement is established in the current evidence.

**Supported assertion:** RC001-AS026

---

## Heat, drought, and scorch

### Expert interpretation

Dark foliage does not make ‘Bloodgood’ immune to:

* leaf scorch;
* heat injury;
* drought stress;
* drying wind;
* or marginal leaf damage.

Good reported summer color retention is an ornamental characteristic, not proof of exceptional physiological resistance.

**Supported assertion:** RC001-AS030

# 11. Cold hardiness

## Published ratings

Missouri Botanical Garden publishes a USDA hardiness range of **zones 5–8** for ‘Bloodgood’.

The cultivar has also been given a United Kingdom RHS hardiness rating in institutional horticultural treatment.

**Supported assertion:** RC001-AS031

## Editorial synthesis

The approved practical formulation is:

> **Commonly listed for USDA zones 5–8, subject to appropriate siting and local winter conditions.**

Hardiness ratings are source-specific climatic estimates and not guarantees of survival.

They do not fully account for:

* winter wind;
* fluctuating temperatures;
* late frost;
* exposed roots;
* container culture;
* poor drainage;
* establishment status;
* or rootstock.

**Supported assertion:** RC001-AS032

# 12. Identification and comparison

## Published comparative context

The principal visible characteristics of ‘Bloodgood’ overlap with other upright red-leaved Japanese maples.

These shared characteristics may include:

* five to seven pointed lobes;
* dark red seasonal foliage;
* upright growth;
* red autumn color;
* and red samaras.

## Editorial synthesis

A probable field identification may be based on the combination of:

* upright to broad-rounded habit;
* non-dissected leaves;
* usually five to seven slender lobes;
* reddish-purple spring growth;
* dark red-purple summer foliage;
* bright red to crimson autumn color;
* red samaras;
* and development into a substantial small tree.

This remains a probable identification, not definitive authentication.

## Expert interpretation

High-confidence identification should combine morphology with provenance and repeated seasonal observation.

Potentially useful future comparative characters include:

* bud-break timing;
* leaf-blade dimensions;
* lobe ratios;
* sinus depth;
* petiole length and color;
* shoot color;
* internode length;
* bud morphology;
* samara measurements;
* bark development;
* and standardized photographs.

Reliable separation from ‘Emperor I’, ‘Fireglow’, heterogeneous ‘Atropurpureum’-type material, and unnamed red seedlings remains unresolved.

**Supported assertions:** RC001-AS038–AS040
**Unresolved records:** RC001-U008–U012

# 13. Practical garden assessment

## Editorial synthesis

‘Bloodgood’ is best understood as a substantial upright red-leaved Japanese maple rather than a compact shrub or laceleaf specimen.

It is particularly suited to gardens seeking:

* persistent dark foliage;
* upright structure;
* a small-tree form;
* broadening crown development;
* and red autumn color.

Its eventual dimensions should be considered before planting near:

* foundations;
* paths;
* fences;
* overhead utilities;
* or restricted planting spaces.

The cultivar’s initially manageable size should not obscure its capacity to become a broad and visually dominant small tree over time.

It is likely to give its best ornamental performance where:

* soil remains moist but well drained;
* roots are not exposed to chronic heat and drought;
* and foliage is protected from the most severe combination of afternoon heat and drying wind.

This final sentence represents broader Japanese-maple horticultural interpretation rather than evidence from a cultivar-specific physiological trial.

# 19. Reference summary

## Editorial synthesis

*‘Bloodgood’ is a widely cultivated upright red-leaved cultivar of* Acer palmatum. *It develops into a substantial large shrub or small tree with a broadening or rounded crown, non-dissected leaves normally divided into five to seven slender pointed lobes, dark red-purple growing-season foliage, and bright red to crimson autumn color. Small purplish flowers are followed by red samaras.*

*The cultivar is historically associated with Bloodgood Nursery of Long Island, New York. Secondary evidence indicates that it was sold there before the Second World War, but its precise selector, date of selection, naming history, and original provenance remain unresolved.*

*Its general horticultural characteristics are well supported. Definitive identification against similar upright red-leaved cultivars is not currently possible from appearance alone and should incorporate provenance, repeated observation, and comparative evidence.*
