# 2. Cultivar identity and nomenclature

## Published Description

The accepted Atlas working name is *Acer palmatum* ‘Osakazuki’. The Royal Horticultural Society displays the epithet as ‘Ōsakazuki’ and marks the name accepted. Missouri Botanical Garden and NC State use the unmacronized form ‘Osakazuki’. [RC006-AS001–AS002; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

Kew's Plants of the World Online accepts *Acer palmatum* Thunb. at species rank. That species-level authority supports the botanical placement of the cultivar but does not establish its place, date or mode of origin. [RC006-AS004; SRC-RC-006-004]

## Editorial Synthesis

The Atlas uses ‘Osakazuki’ as the stable working form while preserving ‘Ōsakazuki’ as the RHS display form. This supports repository search and file stability without erasing institutional orthography.

The following identity states must remain separate:

1. documented vegetatively propagated material with a traceable accession or nursery chain;
2. material accessioned or sold under the cultivar name but not independently authenticated;
3. seed-derived offspring of a plant labelled ‘Osakazuki’;
4. unidentified green-leaved Japanese maples with similar autumn colour.

A nursery, herbarium or living-collection label is evidence about the associated record. It is not, without determination history and provenance, conclusive proof that all material in commerce is one authenticated clone. [RC006-AS022–AS023, AS027; SRC-RC-006-005–006]

## Authentication limit

This Reference Standard defines a conservative descriptive and evidentiary profile. It does not certify individual specimens or guarantee commercial clonal uniformity.

# 3. Naming variants and synonyms

## Source-recorded names

The RHS records:

- *Acer palmatum* ‘Taihai’;
- *Acer palmatum* (Heptalobum Group) ‘Osakazuki’;
- *Acer palmatum* (Elegans Group) ‘Osakazuki’.

The RHS horticultural-group text refers to the amoenum group, and Missouri Botanical Garden presents ‘Osakazuki’ as an Amoenum Group cultivar. [RC006-AS003, AS028; SRC-RC-006-001–002]

## Governed treatment

- **‘Osakazuki’** — accepted Atlas working spelling.
- **‘Ōsakazuki’** — RHS display form; retained as an institutional spelling/romanization variant.
- **‘Taihai’** — RHS-recorded synonym; historical identity scope requires further research.
- **Amoenum / Elegans / Heptalobum formulations** — source-specific horticultural classifications, not separate cultivar identities and not proof of authenticated material.
- **‘Ōsakasuki’** — spelling displayed by the JC Raulston Arboretum living-collection record; unresolved and not approved as a synonym. [RC006-AS029; SRC-RC-006-006]

The group labels must not be collapsed into one unquestioned historical or taxonomic claim. The cultivar identity is governed by the accepted epithet; the classification formulations remain separately traceable.

## Name interpretation

Missouri Botanical Garden reports that the name suggests a leaf like a wine cup, associated with the reported cupped leaf base. This remains a source-provided interpretation. No primary Japanese naming statement or original-character analysis was recovered. [RC006-AS024; SRC-RC-006-002]

# 4. Botanical description

## Published Description

Institutional profiles describe a deciduous shrub or small tree with green palmate leaves and marked autumn colour. RHS describes a large rounded deciduous shrub with relatively large seven-lobed leaves. Missouri Botanical Garden describes a vigorous upright tree with a rounded crown. NC State records a rounded shrub or tree. [RC006-AS005–AS006; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

Small flowers are reported as red by RHS and reddish-purple by Missouri Botanical Garden. Red samaras follow. [RC006-AS011–AS012; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

## Editorial Synthesis

> ‘Osakazuki’ is a green-leaved Japanese-maple cultivar developing as a rounded to upright large shrub or small tree. Its relatively large palmate leaves are usually described with seven serrate lobes and remain green through the growing season before turning brilliant orange-scarlet, crimson or red in autumn. Small spring flowers and red samaras provide secondary seasonal characters.

The combination defines a useful horticultural profile but does not authenticate a plant from appearance alone.

## Scope limits

The present evidence does not provide standardized multi-accession data for bud morphology, petiole length, internode length, leaf ratios, samara measurements, age-related bark, rootstock effects or graft-height effects.

# 5. Whole-plant architecture

## Published Description

RHS describes a large shrub of rounded habit and classifies the habit as bushy. Missouri Botanical Garden describes a vigorous upright tree with a rounded crown. NC State records a rounded form and lists both shrub and tree. [RC006-AS005; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

## Editorial Synthesis

> A rounded to upright Japanese maple developing a rounded or broad-rounded crown, capable of expression as a large shrub or small tree.

The terms are compatible:

- **upright** describes the main growth direction;
- **rounded or broad-rounded** describes crown outline;
- **large shrub or small tree** describes horticultural form, not different cultivar identities.

Architecture may be altered by stem number, rootstock, graft height, formative pruning, competition, exposure and plant age. The cultivar should not be described as narrowly columnar, fastigiate, naturally pendulous or intrinsically dwarf.

## Published dimensions

- RHS: 2.5–4 m high and wide, 10–20 years to stated maturity;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: 15–25 ft high and 10–15 ft wide, with a cultivar paragraph reporting 15–18 ft over ten years and possible development to 20–25 ft;
- NC State: 15–24 ft high and 10–15 ft wide;
- JC Raulston: accession-specific measured heights of 11 ft and 15 ft on stated dates. [RC006-AS013–AS016, AS023; SRC-RC-006-001–003, SRC-RC-006-006]

These are contextual estimates and observations, not a universal maximum or annual-growth rule.

# 6. Leaf morphology

## Published Description

RHS describes relatively large, seven-lobed leaves. Missouri Botanical Garden describes large palmate leaves with seven serrate, moderately cut lobes divided slightly more than halfway to the base. NC State describes bright-green seven-lobed leaves with serrated margins. [RC006-AS006–AS007; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

Missouri Botanical Garden additionally reports that the leaf is cupped at the base. [RC006-AS008; SRC-RC-006-002]

## Editorial Synthesis

The controlled descriptive core is:

- simple, palmate leaf;
- usually seven lobes in reviewed cultivar descriptions;
- serrate margins;
- moderate division rather than laceleaf dissection;
- green growing-season pigmentation;
- a source-reported tendency toward a cupped base.

The leaf belongs to a broad large-lobed palmate or amoenum-type horticultural expression rather than the deeply dissected morphology of RC-002 ‘Seiryu’ or RC-004 ‘Crimson Queen’.

## Measurement limitation

No universal leaf-size range is approved because the profiles do not identify accession, sample size, leaf position, developmental stage, protocol, rootstock or environmental context. Seven lobes and a cupped base are useful comparison characters but are not independently diagnostic.

# 7. Seasonal characteristics

## Spring

The reviewed cultivar profiles support green foliage but do not establish one distinctive cultivar-specific spring hue. Early leaf appearance, frost response and exact colour require authenticated seasonal observation before a stronger claim is approved.

## Summer

RHS, Missouri Botanical Garden and NC State consistently describe green foliage through the principal growing season. [RC006-AS009; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

## Autumn

RHS describes brilliant orange-scarlet; Missouri Botanical Garden emphasizes vibrant crimson red; NC State describes brilliant red and orange. [RC006-AS010; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

> Autumn foliage is typically brilliant orange-scarlet to crimson or red. Exact hue, timing, duration and intensity vary with weather, site, plant condition and observation date.

The cultivar must not be represented with one permanent digital colour or a guarantee of identical annual performance.

## Flowers and fruit

Small red to reddish-purple flowers occur in spring, followed by red paired samaras. These contribute to the seasonal profile but overlap broadly with other *Acer palmatum* material. [RC006-AS011–AS012; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

## Winter interest

No strong cultivar-specific winter bark or stem character is established. NC State reports smooth green to light-gray bark, but the evidence does not support using bark as a principal diagnostic.

# 8. Horticultural information

## Evidence-scope rule

This section separates cultivar-profile guidance from broader *Acer palmatum* practice. Recommendations remain climate- and site-dependent.

## Light and exposure

RHS permits full sun or partial shade, recommends shelter and states that leaf colour is best in partial shade. Missouri Botanical Garden permits full sun to part shade but recommends afternoon shade in hotter conditions and avoidance of hot, dry sites. NC State allows full sun through dappled shade and states that best leaf colour occurs in partial shade. [RC006-AS018; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

> Use full sun to partial shade according to regional heat, wind and water availability. In hot-summer climates, protection from intense afternoon sun and drying wind is prudent.

## Soil and moisture

RHS specifies moist but well-drained, acid or neutral conditions. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends moist, organically rich, slightly acidic, well-drained soil. NC State recommends rich, moist, acidic soil while recording adaptation to several soil textures where drainage is suitable. [RC006-AS017; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

> Provide moist but freely draining soil, reliable moisture during establishment and drought, and avoidance of prolonged waterlogging, chronic dryness and heat-reflective sites.

No narrow cultivar-specific pH requirement is established.

## Scorch conflict

NC State uses resistance language for leaf scorch in sun while also listing leaf scorch as a possible problem. RHS and Missouri Botanical Garden warn that heat, exposure and insufficient moisture can cause scorch. [RC006-AS019; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

No absolute scorch-resistance claim is approved. Exposure and moisture are interacting risk factors.

## Hardiness

- RHS: H6;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: USDA zones 5–9;
- NC State: USDA zones 5a–8b. [RC006-AS020; SRC-RC-006-001–003]

These ratings use different frameworks and are not survival guarantees. Winter wind, fluctuating temperature, late frost, container culture, drainage, establishment, rootstock and root exposure remain relevant.

## Growth

Missouri Botanical Garden calls the cultivar vigorous; NC State labels growth medium. These are qualitative classifications, not measured annual increments.

# 9. Historical provenance

## Documented institutional record

RHS herbarium records preserve material under the cultivar name with dated collections including October 1996 and multiple records from 2000 and 2004. [RC006-AS022; SRC-RC-006-005]

JC Raulston Arboretum displays two living-collection accessions, including accession 001085 received in 2000 and planted in 2004. [RC006-AS023; SRC-RC-006-006]

## Editorial Synthesis

The present evidence does **not** establish:

- original selector;
- selection nursery or garden;
- exact place or mode of origin;
- selection date;
- naming author;
- original Japanese written form;
- earliest use of the name;
- earliest catalogue entry;
- first international distribution;
- precise history of ‘Taihai’.

Late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century institutional records demonstrate documented modern usage, not original provenance.

## JC Raulston discrepancy

The JC Raulston page uses ‘Ōsakasuki’. Until the institution confirms whether this is a typographical, accession-label or supported nomenclatural distinction, the Atlas preserves it as unresolved rather than silently normalizing it.

# 10. Propagation and cultivar continuity

## Published Recommendation

RHS recommends propagation by grafting or softwood cuttings. [RC006-AS025; SRC-RC-006-001]

## Editorial Synthesis

Vegetative propagation is required to reproduce named cultivar material with an expectation of clonal continuity. Seed-derived plants are genetically variable and must not automatically inherit the cultivar identity, even where foliage and autumn colour resemble the parent.

Propagation method alone does not authenticate a plant. Stronger identity assessment may require traceable supplier or accession history, documented scion source, established comparison material, repeated seasonal observation and specialist assessment.

No comprehensive study of current commercial clonal uniformity was located. Rootstock, graft height and cultural conditions may modify phenotype without necessarily indicating a different cultivar.

# 11. Diagnostic framework

## Useful descriptive combination

A probable descriptive match may combine:

- rounded to upright architecture with a rounded crown;
- large-shrub or small-tree development;
- relatively large green palmate leaves;
- usually seven serrate, moderately cut lobes;
- a source-reported cupped leaf base;
- green spring and summer foliage;
- brilliant orange-scarlet, crimson or red autumn colour;
- red samaras.

## Authentication limit

The following do not identify the cultivar independently:

- red autumn colour;
- seven lobes;
- green summer foliage;
- rounded habit;
- a cupped leaf base;
- red samaras;
- a nursery, herbarium or living-collection label;
- one photograph. [RC006-AS026–AS027; SRC-RC-006-001–006]

## Comparative evidence needed

Higher-confidence diagnosis requires authenticated comparison with other green-leaved, large-lobed Japanese maples with strong red autumn colour. Useful future data include standardized leaf dimensions, lobe ratios, sinus depth, basal-cupping frequency, petiole characters, bud-break timing, internode length, samara dimensions, age-calibrated crown development and repeated colour-calibrated photographs.

**Descriptive confidence:** high.  
**Authentication from morphology alone:** low to moderate.
