# 2. Cultivar identity and nomenclature

## Published Description

The Royal Horticultural Society accepts *Acer palmatum* ‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’. NC State Extension uses ‘Mikawa yatsubusa’; JC Raulston Arboretum and Chicago Botanic Garden use ‘Mikawa Yatsubusa’. [RC010-AS001–AS004; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

Kew accepts *Acer palmatum* Thunb. at species rank. Species acceptance supports botanical placement but does not establish cultivar origin or clonal history. [RC010-AS005; SRC-RC-010-007]

## Editorial Synthesis

The Atlas accepts:

> *Acer palmatum* ‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’

as the G0-locked working name. Hyphenation, spacing and capitalization differences are preserved as institutional/search variants rather than separate cultivar identities.

The following states must remain distinct:

1. vegetatively propagated material with documented accession or nursery provenance;
2. material labelled with one of the spelling variants but not independently authenticated;
3. seed-derived offspring;
4. other dwarf or yatsubusa-type *Acer palmatum* selections;
5. specimens that appear layered because of pruning, stress or viewing angle rather than cultivar identity.

## Authentication limit

The combination of dwarf scale and layered foliage is distinctive but not independently authenticating. A nursery label, a single branch close-up or one compact plant cannot establish clonal identity.

# 3. Naming variants and synonyms

## Governed forms

- **‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’** — accepted Atlas working form and current RHS display form.
- **‘Mikawa yatsubusa’** — NC State and Maple Society North America form; normalized search variant.
- **‘Mikawa Yatsubusa’** — JC Raulston and Chicago Botanic Garden display form; capitalization variant.
- **Cultivar synonyms** — none approved from the reviewed institutional source set.

## Name interpretation

NC State reports that the name means “a small cluster of three rivers.” This is retained as a source-reported interpretation only. No primary Japanese naming statement, original characters or historical linguistic analysis was recovered. [RC010-AS006; SRC-RC-010-002]

Commercial accounts that infer a specific wild origin in the former Mikawa/Tōkai region or a 1970s discovery remain research leads, not approved provenance, because no primary catalogue, registration record or traceable original account was recovered.

## Editorial rule

Search normalization may remove hyphens or standardize capitalization, but the Atlas must preserve the source form and must not convert orthographic variation into multiple cultivar entities.

# 4. Botanical description

## Published Description

RHS describes a compact, multibranched, slow-growing small tree with dense, tightly overlapping five- or seven-lobed leaves creating a bunched, layered effect. NC State describes a very small compact, dense, multibranched tree with layered foliage and an almost contorted appearance. Chicago Botanic Garden describes deeply lobed elongated foliage forming overlapping tufts and small “canopies” on each branch. [RC010-AS007–AS012; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

## Editorial Synthesis

> ‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’ is a slow-growing dwarf Japanese maple with a dense multibranched framework and tightly overlapping palmate leaves. The close leaf arrangement produces layered, tufted or miniature-canopy effects along branches. Spring foliage is yellow-green, summer foliage medium to bright green and autumn foliage ranges through gold, orange, scarlet and red.

The cultivar's principal diagnostic effect is produced by architecture and leaf arrangement together. It must not be reduced to an isolated leaf outline.

## Scope limits

The reviewed evidence does not provide standardized authenticated multi-accession measurements for:

- internode length;
- overlap angle and leaf spacing;
- number of leaves per branch interval;
- leaf size by position and age;
- crown density by age;
- degree of contortion;
- rootstock or graft-height effects;
- or standardized comparisons with RC-008 ‘Shishi-gashira’.

# 5. Whole-plant architecture

## Published Description

RHS describes a compact, multibranched, small tree with spreading branches and dwarf-group classification. NC State describes a dense, multistemmed tree. Chicago Botanic Garden records an upright plant shape. JC Raulston records tree form, a general 3–4 ft size and a documented accession reaching 6 ft. [RC010-AS007, RC010-AS013–AS018; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

## Editorial Synthesis

The normalized architecture is:

> A very compact, slow-growing, dense and multibranched dwarf shrub or small tree, with spreading branch development possible within an overall upright-to-rounded framework.

The source terms are not treated as exact contradictions:

- **upright** describes principal vertical development;
- **spreading branched** describes lateral branch behavior;
- **dense/multibranched** describes crown construction;
- **dwarf** describes relative scale and slow development, not an immutable maximum height.

## Published dimensions

- RHS: 1–1.5 m high and wide, with a narrative estimate of approximately 1–1.2 m and 5–10 years to stated maturity;
- NC State: 4–6 ft high and approximately 4 ft wide;
- Chicago Botanic Garden archived profile: 5 ft in 10 years;
- JC Raulston summary: 3–4 ft, but accession 030154, received in 2003 and planted in 2004, measured 6 ft on 8 June 2022. [RC010-AS013–AS018; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

These source frameworks demonstrate that “dwarf” is relative. No universal mature ceiling or annual growth increment is approved.

# 6. Leaf morphology

## Published Description

RHS reports tightly overlapping leaves with five or seven lobes. NC State reports deeply lobed, serrated leaves and a structured field of five to nine lobes, while Chicago Botanic Garden also reports five to nine lobes and describes elongated, overlapping foliage. [RC010-AS008–AS012; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

## Editorial Synthesis

The controlled leaf and arrangement profile is:

- simple, opposite, palmate leaf;
- five to seven lobes in RHS, with five to nine in the North American institutional profiles;
- serrated margins;
- green growing-season pigmentation;
- close overlap among adjacent leaves;
- layered, tufted or miniature-canopy presentation along branches.

The North Carolina structured leaf fields may include inherited species-level morphology. The draft therefore treats the detailed 2–5-inch size and five-to-nine-lobe range as institutional report, not a universal cultivar measurement.

## Diagnostic distinction from RC-008

RC-008 ‘Shishi-gashira’ is defined by small crinkled leaves held in bunches on a denser upright framework. RC-010 is defined more strongly by planar or shingle-like overlap and layered canopy effects on a lower dwarf framework. This distinction is an editorial synthesis requiring same-scale authenticated comparison.

# 7. Seasonal characteristics

## Spring

RHS reports pale yellow-green foliage. NC State reports yellow-green leaves and Chicago Botanic Garden reports yellow-tinted green foliage emerging early. [RC010-AS019; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Spring foliage is pale to yellow-green. Early emergence is reported by Chicago Botanic Garden but requires climate-qualified observation.

## Summer

RHS reports bright green and NC State/Chicago Botanic Garden report medium to rich mid-green. [RC010-AS020; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Summer foliage is medium to bright green, with the layered architecture remaining the principal diagnostic feature.

## Autumn

RHS reports orange, gold and red. NC State reports yellow-gold with the serrated edges of outer leaves becoming bright red, while its structured fields also include orange and red/burgundy. Chicago Botanic Garden reports bright orange to scarlet and a broader yellow-red-purple range. [RC010-AS021–AS023; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Autumn foliage ranges from yellow-gold through orange and scarlet to red, with red edging potentially conspicuous on outer leaves. Purple is retained only as a source-reported broader possibility, not a defining universal state.

## Flowers and fruit

RHS reports inconspicuous small green flowers and red-flushed winged seeds. Chicago Botanic Garden reports small reddish-purple flowers in May and June and rich-red mature samaras. [RC010-AS024–AS025; SRC-RC-010-001, SRC-RC-010-004]

The flower-colour discrepancy is preserved. Flowers and fruit are secondary characters and do not independently authenticate the cultivar.

## Winter interest

RHS reports green bark; NC State reports green stems and green to light-brown bark. The compact branch framework and green young wood may provide winter interest, but no unique bark diagnostic is approved. [RC010-AS026; SRC-RC-010-001–002]

# 8. Horticultural information

## Light and exposure

RHS permits full sun and partial shade, while recommending a sheltered position and reliable moisture. NC State permits partial shade to full sun. JC Raulston lists light shade. Chicago Botanic Garden permits full sun to partial shade. [RC010-AS027; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

**Editorial synthesis:**

> Use partial shade to full sun according to regional heat, wind and water availability. Light shade is a prudent default where summer exposure is intense, and full sun requires reliable moisture.

## Soil and moisture

RHS recommends moisture-retentive but well-drained soil and lists acid, neutral or alkaline pH. NC State recommends moist, well-drained acid to neutral soil across several textures. Chicago Botanic Garden lists moist soil. [RC010-AS028; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

**Editorial synthesis:**

> Provide moisture-retentive but freely draining soil. The broader RHS pH range is preserved as a source report; no universal alkaline tolerance or narrow pH requirement is approved.

## Heat, scorch and temperature risk

NC State describes greater heat tolerance than most Japanese maples but also warns that temperature extremes, wind, drought and underwatering can damage foliage or cause scorch. RHS likewise warns of scorch from moisture deficit or excessive exposure. [RC010-AS029–AS031; SRC-RC-010-001–002]

**Governed treatment:** the source-reported relative heat tolerance is retained with moderate confidence and must not be converted into heat immunity, drought tolerance or scorch resistance.

## Hardiness

- RHS: H5;
- NC State: USDA zones 5b–9b;
- archived Chicago Botanic Garden: zones 6–9, with protected-site caution in zone 5. [RC010-AS032; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

These are source-specific frameworks and are not guarantees.

## Container and bonsai use

RHS supports containers and bonsai; NC State and Chicago Botanic Garden likewise recommend small-space, container or bonsai use. These are horticultural use recommendations, not identity evidence. [RC010-AS033; SRC-RC-010-001–004]

# 9. Historical provenance

## Documented institutional record

JC Raulston accession 030154 was received as a one-gallon plant on 22 January 2003, planted on 17 May 2004 and measured at 6 ft on 8 June 2022. [RC010-AS017; SRC-RC-010-003]

JC Raulston's photograph collection contains twenty-four records under the cultivar name, with dates from 6 April 2005 through 28 March 2026 and additional autumn records from 2007 and 2024. [RC010-AS034; SRC-RC-010-006]

Maple Society North America records *Acer palmatum* ‘Mikawa yatsubusa’ as its 2015 Maple of the Year, the first year of that award programme. [RC010-AS035; SRC-RC-010-005]

## Editorial Synthesis

These sources establish modern institutional cultivation and horticultural recognition. They do not establish:

- original selector;
- selection site;
- mode of origin;
- selection date;
- naming author;
- earliest catalogue or registration;
- original Japanese written form;
- or first international distribution.

## Rejected historical shortcut

Commercial claims that the cultivar was discovered wild in the Tōkai/Mikawa region in the 1970s are not included in the approved historical core without a primary or authoritative traceable locator.

# 10. Propagation and cultivar continuity

## Published Recommendation

RHS recommends layering in autumn, grafting in late winter or softwood cuttings. [RC010-AS036; SRC-RC-010-001]

## Editorial Synthesis

Vegetative propagation is required to maintain the named cultivar with an expectation of continuity. Seed-derived offspring must not automatically inherit the cultivar identity, even when compact or layered features recur.

The NC State statement that the plant “self-seeds easily” describes reproductive or landscape behavior, not clonal continuity. Seedlings are genetically distinct and must not be published as ‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’ without separate cultivar establishment. [RC010-AS037; SRC-RC-010-002]

Propagation method alone does not authenticate a specimen. Stronger assessment requires traceable scion/accession history, repeated observation and comparison with documented material.

## Continuity uncertainty

No comprehensive study of current commercial clonal uniformity, seedling resemblance, rootstock effects or synonym-line variation was located.

# 11. Diagnostic framework

## Useful descriptive combination

A probable descriptive match may combine:

- slow-growing dwarf scale;
- dense multibranched architecture;
- compact upright-to-rounded or spreading-branched framework;
- tightly overlapping palmate leaves;
- layered, tufted or small-canopy branch effects;
- yellow-green spring foliage;
- medium to bright-green summer foliage;
- and yellow-gold, orange, scarlet or red autumn expression.

## Authentication limit

The following do not independently authenticate ‘Mikawa-yatsubusa’:

- one overlapping branch spray;
- compact size;
- one yellow-green spring leaf;
- red-edged autumn foliage;
- a bonsai presentation;
- a nursery label;
- an institutional photograph caption;
- or a seedling from a labelled parent. [RC010-AS038–AS040; SRC-RC-010-001–007]

## Confusion risks

- RC-008 ‘Shishi-gashira’, whose dense effect derives more strongly from small crinkled leaves and short internodes;
- other yatsubusa or dwarf *Acer palmatum* cultivars;
- plants shaped by bonsai pruning;
- branch photographs that hide whole-plant scale;
- seedlings selected from a labelled parent;
- stress-induced compactness or distorted foliage.

## Comparative evidence needed

Higher-confidence diagnosis requires standardized comparison of internode length, leaf size, lobe number, overlap angle, crown height/spread, seasonal colour, bud-break timing and same-scale branch architecture against RC-008 and other dwarf cultivars.

## Diagnostic conclusion

**Descriptive confidence:** high.  
**Authentication from morphology alone:** low to moderate.
