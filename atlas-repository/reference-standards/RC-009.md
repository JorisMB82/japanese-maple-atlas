# 2. Cultivar identity and nomenclature

## Published Description

The Royal Horticultural Society and Missouri Botanical Garden use *Acer palmatum* ‘Katsura’. RHS marks the name accepted. Kew accepts *Acer palmatum* Thunb. at species rank. [RC009-AS001–AS003; SRC-RC-009-001–003]

## Editorial Synthesis

The Atlas accepts:

> *Acer palmatum* ‘Katsura’

as the stable working name. No cultivar synonym is approved in this draft because the reviewed institutional profiles do not provide one.

Identity states must remain distinct:

1. vegetatively propagated material with traceable provenance;
2. material labelled ‘Katsura’ without independent authentication;
3. seed-derived offspring;
4. other early-emerging yellow/orange *Acer palmatum* cultivars;
5. the unrelated katsura tree, *Cercidiphyllum japonicum*.

## Authentication limit

The cultivar's spring colour trajectory is highly descriptive but not independently authenticating. A nursery label, early leaf colour or one photograph cannot establish clonal identity.

# 3. Naming variants and synonyms

## Governed name position

- **‘Katsura’** — accepted Atlas working epithet.
- **Cultivar synonyms** — none approved from the current institutional source set.
- **Case, quotation and italicization variants** — normalized editorially; they do not create separate identities.

## Cross-taxon confusion

“Katsura” is also the English common name used for *Cercidiphyllum japonicum*, a taxonomically unrelated tree. Missouri Botanical Garden search results present both *Acer palmatum* ‘Katsura’ and *Cercidiphyllum japonicum* as separate records. [RC009-AS004; SRC-RC-009-002, SRC-RC-009-006]

The Atlas must require the botanical combination in identity-sensitive contexts and should not allow search results for the unrelated tree to populate cultivar history, morphology or cultivation.

## Historical and linguistic limit

No primary naming statement or original Japanese historical source was recovered. The Atlas does not approve a translation, naming rationale or geographic inference from the epithet.

# 4. Botanical description

## Published Description

RHS describes an upright deciduous shrub with five- to seven-lobed yellow-orange foliage with pink margins in spring, green summer foliage and bright-orange autumn colour. Missouri Botanical Garden describes a dense compact cultivar with small five-lobed leaves to approximately 1 inch long, lanceolate lobes, slender tips and shallow-toothed margins. [RC009-AS005–AS010; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

Small red or reddish spring flowers are reported. [RC009-AS011; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

## Editorial Synthesis

> ‘Katsura’ is a compact upright Japanese maple distinguished by small palmate foliage that emerges chartreuse to yellow-orange with orange or pink margins, becomes bright green in summer and turns orange to yellow in autumn. Young plants may be vase-shaped and broaden into a rounded crown with age.

The cultivar's primary product role is temporal: its most distinctive expression occurs during early leaf emergence.

## Scope limits

The current evidence does not provide standardized authenticated multi-accession measurements for:

- exact bud-break date across climates;
- duration of spring margin colour;
- frequency and width of pink versus orange margins;
- leaf measurements beyond the Missouri estimate;
- petiole colour and length;
- shoot and internode dimensions;
- autumn-colour duration;
- or rootstock and graft-height effects.

# 5. Whole-plant architecture

## Published Description

RHS calls the plant an upright deciduous shrub and classifies the habit as bushy. Missouri Botanical Garden describes a dense, compact cultivar that is vase-shaped when young, reaching 4–6 ft over the first ten years, then becoming more rounded and maturing to 10–12 ft. [RC009-AS005, RC009-AS012–AS015; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

## Editorial Synthesis

The normalized architecture is:

> A dense, compact and upright to vase-shaped shrub or small tree, becoming more rounded with age.

The terms represent development over time rather than competing identities:

- **upright/vase-shaped** — juvenile or early crown direction;
- **dense/compact** — crown and growth character;
- **rounded** — mature crown tendency;
- **shrub or small tree** — horticultural expression.

## Published dimensions

- RHS: 2.5–4 m high and wide, with 20–50 years to stated maturity;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: 4–6 ft after approximately ten years, ultimately 10–12 ft high and wide. [RC009-AS012–AS015; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

These are contextual estimates. No universal age-size curve, annual increment or mature maximum is approved.

# 6. Leaf morphology

## Published Description

RHS describes five- to seven-lobed foliage. Missouri Botanical Garden describes small palmate leaves, usually five-lobed and up to approximately 1 inch long, with lanceolate lobes, slender tips and shallow-toothed margins. [RC009-AS006–AS008; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

## Editorial Synthesis

The controlled profile is:

- simple palmate leaf;
- five lobes in the detailed Missouri cultivar description, with five to seven accepted in RHS;
- small blade;
- lanceolate lobes with slender tips;
- shallow-toothed margins;
- spring colour concentrated in the blade and margin trajectory rather than structural variegation.

The orange/pink spring edge must not be confused with the persistent cream-white marginal variegation of RC-007 ‘Butterfly’.

## Measurement limit

One universal leaf size or lobe count is not approved. Leaf position, developmental stage, climate and plant vigour may affect expression.

# 7. Seasonal characteristics

## Spring

RHS reports yellow-orange foliage with pink margins. Missouri Botanical Garden reports chartreuse emergence with orange-tinted margins. Both report small red to reddish flowers. [RC009-AS009–AS011; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Spring foliage emerges chartreuse to yellow-orange with orange or pink margins. This is the cultivar's strongest descriptive season, but timing, margin width and pigment intensity vary.

Missouri Botanical Garden reports early leaf-out and vulnerability to late spring frost. [RC009-AS016; SRC-RC-009-002]

## Summer

RHS and Missouri Botanical Garden state that foliage matures to green or bright green. [RC009-AS017; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Summer foliage is bright green, and the cultivar may become less visually distinctive from other green-leaved maples after the spring colour fades.

## Autumn

RHS describes bright orange. Missouri Botanical Garden describes pastel shades of orange and yellow. [RC009-AS018; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Autumn foliage turns orange to yellow, ranging from bright to pastel expression according to season, site and plant condition.

## Flowers and fruit

Small red to reddish flowers occur in spring and are weak diagnostic characters. No strong cultivar-specific fruit description is approved.

## Winter interest

No cultivar-specific bark or stem diagnostic is established.

# 8. Horticultural information

## Light and exposure

RHS permits full sun or partial shade and recommends shelter. Missouri Botanical Garden permits full sun to part shade, with afternoon shade preferred in hotter southern conditions. [RC009-AS019; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

**Editorial synthesis:**

> Use full sun to partial shade according to regional heat and moisture. Partial shade and protection from hot afternoon exposure are prudent where summers are intense.

## Soil and moisture

RHS recommends moist but well-drained acid or neutral soil. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends moist, organically rich, slightly acidic and well-drained soil and reports chlorosis risk in high-pH conditions. [RC009-AS020–AS021; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

## Scorch and frost

RHS and Missouri Botanical Garden warn that heat, excessive exposure and insufficient moisture can cause scorch. Missouri also reports early leaf-out and late-spring-frost risk. [RC009-AS016, RC009-AS022; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

The Atlas rejects absolute sun-tolerance, scorch-resistance and frost-immunity claims.

## Hardiness

- RHS: H6;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: USDA zones 5–9. [RC009-AS023; SRC-RC-009-001–002]

These are different rating systems and are not guarantees.

## Propagation

RHS recommends grafting or softwood cuttings. Vegetative propagation is required for cultivar continuity; seed-derived offspring must not inherit the name automatically. [RC009-AS024; SRC-RC-009-001]

# 10. Propagation and cultivar continuity

## Published Recommendation

RHS recommends grafting or softwood cuttings. [RC009-AS024; SRC-RC-009-001]

## Editorial Synthesis

Vegetative propagation supports continuity but does not itself authenticate a plant. Identity assessment should consider scion source, accession or supplier history, repeated seasonal observation and comparison with documented material.

Seed-derived plants must not automatically inherit the cultivar identity, even if early foliage resembles ‘Katsura’.

## Continuity uncertainty

No comprehensive study of current commercial clonal uniformity, rootstock effects or variation in spring colour duration was located.

# 11. Diagnostic framework

## Useful descriptive combination

A probable descriptive match may combine:

- dense compact upright or vase-shaped juvenile habit;
- crown becoming rounder with age;
- small five- to seven-lobed palmate leaves;
- chartreuse to yellow-orange spring emergence;
- orange or pink spring margins;
- bright-green summer foliage;
- orange to yellow autumn colour;
- and early leaf-out.

## Authentication limit

The following do not independently authenticate ‘Katsura’:

- one yellow-orange spring leaf;
- pink or orange margins;
- early bud break;
- a compact vase-shaped plant;
- orange autumn colour;
- a nursery label;
- or one photograph. [RC009-AS027–AS029; SRC-RC-009-001–006]

## Confusion risks

- *Cercidiphyllum japonicum*, the unrelated katsura tree;
- RC-005 ‘Aureum’, with more sustained yellow/yellow-green foliage under *Acer shirasawanum*;
- RC-007 ‘Butterfly’, with persistent structural cream-white marginal variegation;
- other early-emerging yellow/orange *Acer palmatum* cultivars;
- frost-damaged spring foliage;
- photographs taken after the defining spring phase.

## Comparative evidence needed

Higher-confidence diagnosis requires standardized observation of bud-break timing, leaf dimensions, margin pigmentation, duration of spring colour, summer transition and same-scale comparisons with ‘Aureum’, ‘Butterfly’ and other spring-colour cultivars.

## Diagnostic conclusion

**Descriptive confidence:** high during spring emergence.  
**Authentication from appearance alone:** low to moderate.
