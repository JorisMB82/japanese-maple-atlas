# 2. Cultivar identity and nomenclature

## Published Description

The Royal Horticultural Society maintains an accepted profile under *Acer palmatum* ‘Butterfly’ and records *Acer palmatum* ‘Kochō-no-mai’ as a synonym. A separate RHS page uses ‘Kochō-no-mai’ as the page heading while presenting the common horticultural name “Japanese maple ‘Butterfly’.” [RC007-AS001–AS003; SRC-RC-007-001]

Missouri Botanical Garden and the University of Connecticut Plant Database use *Acer palmatum* ‘Butterfly’. Kew accepts *Acer palmatum* Thunb. at species rank. Kew's species treatment supports botanical placement but does not establish the cultivar's origin or clonal history. [RC007-AS001, RC007-AS004; SRC-RC-007-002–004]

## Editorial Synthesis

The Atlas accepts:

> *Acer palmatum* ‘Butterfly’

as the stable working name. The RHS-recorded ‘Kochō-no-mai’ relationship is preserved as institutional synonym evidence, while its historical origin, original written form and sequence of use remain unresolved.

The following identity states must remain distinct:

1. vegetatively propagated material with a documented accession or nursery chain;
2. material labelled ‘Butterfly’ or ‘Kochō-no-mai’ without independent authentication;
3. seed-derived offspring of a labelled plant;
4. other marginally variegated *Acer palmatum* cultivars;
5. green shoots arising within a variegated plant.

A cultivar label, herbarium label or institutional photograph record is evidence about the associated record. It is not by itself conclusive proof that all plants carrying the name are one authenticated clone.

## Authentication limit

This Reference Standard defines a conservative descriptive and evidentiary profile. It does not authenticate individual plants, certify commercial clonal uniformity or establish that every plant sold under either recorded name has an identical provenance chain.

# 3. Naming variants and synonyms

## Source-recorded names

RHS records:

- *Acer palmatum* ‘Butterfly’ as an accepted profile name;
- *Acer palmatum* ‘Kochō-no-mai’ as a synonym;
- a separate accepted-name page headed ‘Kochō-no-mai’ that identifies the plant as Japanese maple ‘Butterfly’. [RC007-AS002–AS003; SRC-RC-007-001]

## Governed treatment

- **‘Butterfly’** — accepted Atlas working epithet.
- **‘Kochō-no-mai’** — RHS-recorded synonym and institutional display form; historical equivalence is accepted provisionally for current horticultural use but its primary nomenclatural history remains unresolved.
- **‘Kocho-no-mai’ / ‘Kocho no mai’** — unaccented or differently punctuated search variants; not separately governed cultivar identities.
- **‘White Butterfly’** — a separately accepted RHS cultivar name. It must not be collapsed into ‘Butterfly’ without independent evidence. [RC007-AS005; SRC-RC-007-007]
- **‘Kocho Nishiki’ and similar commercial claims** — unresolved research leads, not approved synonyms in this draft.

## Editorial Synthesis

The Atlas should store the accepted display name and the RHS synonym relationship separately from spelling normalization. Macron loss, punctuation variation and word spacing may support search, but they do not create additional cultivar entities.

## Name-history limit

No primary Japanese catalogue, registration record or original naming statement was recovered in this research cycle. The Atlas therefore does not approve a literal translation, naming author, introduction date or historical sequence between ‘Butterfly’ and ‘Kochō-no-mai’.

# 4. Botanical description

## Published Description

RHS describes a rather upright shrub or small tree with five- to seven-lobed leaves irregularly margined with creamy white and often tinged pink. UConn calls it a true variegated form with grey-green leaves carrying white and pink margins. Missouri Botanical Garden treats it as a small tree and reports reddish-purple spring flowers. [RC007-AS006–AS010; SRC-RC-007-001–003]

## Editorial Synthesis

> ‘Butterfly’ is an upright to bushy, relatively slender Japanese-maple cultivar distinguished by palmate five- to seven-lobed leaves with green to grey-green centres and irregular cream-white marginal variegation, frequently showing pink tinting. Small spring flowers are secondary characters.

The irregularity of the marginal pattern is part of the descriptive profile. The record should not imply that every leaf carries an identical width, shape or proportion of white tissue.

## Scope limits

The current evidence does not provide standardized, authenticated multi-accession measurements for:

- leaf length and width;
- margin width as a percentage of blade area;
- frequency of pink tinting by season;
- shoot-to-shoot variegation stability;
- internode length;
- bud morphology;
- flower or samara measurements;
- rootstock and graft-height effects;
- or quantified reversion frequency.

# 5. Whole-plant architecture

## Published Description

RHS describes the cultivar as a rather upright shrub or small tree and classifies the habit as bushy. Missouri Botanical Garden publishes a height of 7–12 ft and spread of 4–8 ft. UConn describes a slow grower reaching approximately 15 ft with age. [RC007-AS006, RC007-AS011–AS013; SRC-RC-007-001–003]

## Editorial Synthesis

The normalized architectural description is:

> A rather upright, bushy deciduous shrub or small tree, commonly narrower than tall within the reviewed size frameworks.

The terms are compatible:

- **upright** describes the principal growth direction;
- **bushy** describes branching density and crown texture;
- **shrub or small tree** describes horticultural expression rather than separate identities.

Architecture may be changed by stem number, rootstock, graft height, pruning, light direction, exposure, competition and age. The cultivar should not be described as naturally cascading, strongly pendulous, columnar or intrinsically dwarf.

## Published dimensions

- RHS: 2.5–4 m ultimate height and 1–1.5 m spread, with 10–20 years to stated maturity;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: 7–12 ft high and 4–8 ft wide;
- UConn: approximately 15 ft with age and slow growth. [RC007-AS011–AS013; SRC-RC-007-001–003]

These are source-specific horticultural estimates. The Atlas does not approve one universal mature maximum, crown ratio or annual growth increment.

# 6. Leaf morphology

## Published Description

RHS describes five- to seven-lobed leaves with irregular creamy-white margins, often tinged pink. UConn describes grey-green leaves with white and pink margins. Both support marginal rather than central or diffuse variegation as the principal leaf character. [RC007-AS007–AS009; SRC-RC-007-001, SRC-RC-007-003]

## Editorial Synthesis

The controlled descriptive core is:

- simple, palmate leaves;
- five to seven lobes in the RHS cultivar description;
- green to grey-green central tissue;
- irregular cream-white marginal tissue;
- frequent pink tinting, especially useful as a seasonal descriptive character;
- palmatum-group rather than finely dissected laceleaf morphology.

The irregular margin may differ among leaves and shoots. Photography, sun exposure, leaf age, stress and colour processing may materially affect the apparent contrast between green, cream and pink areas.

## Variegation and reversion limit

Variegated Japanese maples may be managed for non-variegated shoots in horticultural practice, but the reviewed institutional cultivar profiles do not quantify reversion frequency for ‘Butterfly’. This draft therefore treats reversion as a research and management question rather than a proven constant diagnostic event.

A wholly green shoot observed on a labelled plant must not automatically be named as a separate cultivar. Conversely, one variegated leaf is not sufficient to authenticate ‘Butterfly’.

# 7. Seasonal characteristics

## Spring

RHS describes cream and green variegated foliage and states that the margins are often tinged pink. Small red flowers may occur. Missouri Botanical Garden reports reddish-purple bloom in April. [RC007-AS009–AS010, RC007-AS014; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Spring foliage is green to grey-green with irregular cream-white margins, often pink-tinged. Pink intensity and distribution should be treated as variable rather than guaranteed.

## Summer

RHS continues to describe cream and green variegated foliage through summer, while UConn emphasizes the grey-green centre and white/pink margin. [RC007-AS009, RC007-AS015; SRC-RC-007-001, SRC-RC-007-003]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Summer foliage remains marginally variegated, with green to grey-green centres and cream-white margins; residual pink may vary with leaf age and conditions.

## Autumn

The RHS seasonal table continues to display cream, green and variegated foliage in autumn, and Missouri Botanical Garden flags the plant as having good fall foliage. The reviewed institutional sources do not establish one precise cultivar-specific autumn colour sequence. [RC007-AS016; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

**Governed synthesis:**

> Autumn expression is insufficiently characterized for a stronger colour claim in this draft. The variegated pattern may remain visible, but one universal pink, red, orange or yellow trajectory is not approved.

## Flowers and fruit

RHS reports occasional small red spring flowers; Missouri Botanical Garden reports reddish-purple April bloom. These are weak diagnostic characters. No cultivar-specific samara description strong enough for identification is approved. [RC007-AS010, RC007-AS014; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

## Winter interest

No cultivar-specific winter bark or stem colour is established in the reviewed evidence.

# 8. Horticultural information

## Evidence-scope rule

Cultivar-specific profile guidance is used where available. Species-level guidance from UConn and Kew is not automatically converted into cultivar-specific tolerance claims.

## Light and exposure

RHS recommends partial shade and sheltered exposure, states that leaf colour is best in partial shade and allows full sun where conditions permit. Missouri Botanical Garden permits full sun to part shade but recommends afternoon shade in hotter southern conditions. [RC007-AS017; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

**Editorial synthesis:**

> Partial shade is the preferred general exposure. Full sun may be suitable in cooler regions or reliably moist sites, but intense heat, drying wind and moisture deficit increase scorch risk and can degrade variegated tissue.

## Soil and moisture

RHS recommends moist but well-drained acid or neutral soil. Missouri Botanical Garden recommends moist, organically rich, slightly acidic and well-drained soil. [RC007-AS018; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

**Editorial synthesis:**

> Provide moist but freely draining soil, maintain reliable moisture during establishment and hot weather, and avoid prolonged waterlogging, chronic drought and strongly heat-reflective sites.

## Scorch and variegated tissue

RHS states that leaf scorch can result from lack of soil moisture or excessive exposure. Missouri Botanical Garden warns that new foliage may scorch in hot full-sun locations, particularly when soil moisture is inconsistent. [RC007-AS019; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

The Atlas does not approve an absolute sun-tolerance or scorch-resistance claim. Cream-white marginal tissue may visually expose damage more clearly than green tissue, but a cultivar-specific physiological comparison has not been established.

## Hardiness

- RHS: H6 under the RHS framework;
- Missouri Botanical Garden: USDA zones 5–8. [RC007-AS020; SRC-RC-007-001–002]

These systems are source-specific and are not directly interchangeable. Winter wind, fluctuating temperatures, late frost, drainage, container culture, root exposure, rootstock and establishment status remain material.

## Growth and maintenance

UConn describes slow growth. RHS places the cultivar in pruning group 1 and recommends only the ordinary limited pruning approach associated with that group. No universal annual increment is approved. [RC007-AS013, RC007-AS021; SRC-RC-007-001, SRC-RC-007-003]

Non-variegated shoots should be documented before removal and must not be used to infer a separate cultivar identity without evidence. This is a management precaution, not an approved quantified reversion claim.

# 9. Historical provenance

## Documented institutional record

RHS herbarium records preserve material under the cultivar name with collection dates of 14 April 2000, 6 June 2000 and 18 October 2000. The records identify RHS 1851 Royal Commission Herbarium custody and RHS copyright. [RC007-AS022; SRC-RC-007-005]

The JC Raulston Arboretum photograph collection contains eleven records under *Acer palmatum* ‘Butterfly’, dated from 15 October 2001 through 9 July 2014 and associated with several institutional or private-garden locations. [RC007-AS023; SRC-RC-007-006]

## Editorial Synthesis

These records establish documented modern institutional use of the name. They do not establish:

- original selector;
- selection site;
- selection date;
- naming author;
- original written Japanese form;
- earliest catalogue entry;
- first international distribution;
- or the primary historical basis for the ‘Kochō-no-mai’ synonym.

## Rejected historical shortcut

Commercial claims that the cultivar was known by a particular nineteenth-century date or distributed by a named Japanese nurseryman in a stated year remain research leads only. No primary catalogue, registration record or page-level specialist reference was recovered to support those claims in this cycle.

## Institutional-record limit

Herbarium and photograph records demonstrate the existence and labelling of particular records at particular dates. They do not independently prove that the material represents the original clone or that every current commercial plant is clonally identical.

# 10. Propagation and cultivar continuity

## Published Recommendation

RHS recommends propagation by grafting or softwood cuttings. UConn states that cultivars are generally grafted and may also be propagated by softwood stem cuttings. [RC007-AS024; SRC-RC-007-001, SRC-RC-007-003]

## Editorial Synthesis

Vegetative propagation is required to maintain the named cultivar with an expectation of continuity. Seed-derived plants are genetically variable and must not automatically inherit the cultivar identity, even where marginal variegation appears.

Propagation method alone does not authenticate a plant. Stronger identity assessment may require:

- traceable scion or accession provenance;
- documented supplier history;
- comparison with established material;
- repeated seasonal observation;
- and specialist review where material claims conflict.

## Variegation continuity

The present source set does not quantify the stability of marginal variegation across propagation lines, rootstocks, climates or plant ages. Commercial uniformity therefore remains unresolved.

# 11. Diagnostic framework

## Useful descriptive combination

A probable descriptive match may combine:

- rather upright, bushy shrub or small-tree architecture;
- a relatively slender crown within source-published size frameworks;
- five- to seven-lobed palmate leaves;
- green to grey-green centres;
- irregular cream-white margins;
- frequent pink tinting;
- and continuity of the marginal pattern through the growing season.

## Authentication limit

The following do not independently authenticate ‘Butterfly’:

- one white-margined leaf;
- pink spring tinting;
- an upright habit;
- a nursery label;
- an institutional photograph caption;
- a herbarium label;
- or an unverified commercial synonym. [RC007-AS025–AS027; SRC-RC-007-001–007]

## Confusion risks

The principal confusion risks include:

- the separately accepted *Acer palmatum* ‘White Butterfly’;
- other marginally variegated Japanese-maple cultivars;
- environmentally damaged or chlorotic leaves;
- overexposed photographs that erase cream/green boundaries;
- non-variegated shoots on a labelled plant;
- and search results for unrelated plants using “Butterfly” or “Kochō-no-mai.”

## Comparative evidence needed

Higher-confidence diagnosis would benefit from authenticated comparison of:

- margin pattern and width;
- leaf size and lobe ratios;
- petiole colour and length;
- spring pink distribution;
- summer persistence of white tissue;
- autumn transition;
- shoot-level variegation stability;
- and crown development by age.

## Diagnostic conclusion

**Descriptive confidence:** high.  
**Authentication from appearance alone:** low to moderate.

# 17. Rejected claims

1. One white-margined leaf identifies ‘Butterfly’.
2. Pink spring tinting independently authenticates the cultivar.
3. Every plant labelled ‘Kochō-no-mai’ is proven to share one documented clonal chain with every ‘Butterfly’.
4. ‘White Butterfly’ is a synonym of ‘Butterfly’.
5. ‘Kocho Nishiki’ is an approved synonym.
6. A commercial statement about an 1882 origin or a 1938 distributor is established history without a primary locator.
7. Every leaf has an identical white-margin width.
8. The plant always reverts at a predictable frequency.
9. A green shoot is automatically a new cultivar.
10. The cultivar has one fixed mature height or annual growth rate.
11. Full sun cannot cause scorch.
12. Autumn foliage always follows one exact colour sequence.
13. An institutional photograph caption or herbarium label conclusively authenticates the clone.
14. Seedlings inherit the named cultivar identity.
15. Variegation visible in an overexposed photograph is reliable diagnostic evidence.
16. An online image can be reused without a separate rights determination.
